Freedom to Speak to Myself

Jun 2026 · 4 min read

I grow. I find I can speak — memories, experiences, technicality, work, the books I read, the fights I've had, the people I've sat with in California. The thoughts that come naturally. That's what I want to protect.

The Thought That Belongs to Me

There is a specific feeling that happens when a thought forms that is completely yours. Not a thought you performed for an audience. Not a thought shaped by the awareness of being watched. Just a thing that arrived — a memory, a connection, an idea that grew from experience and silence and reading and living.

I found this late. Or maybe I found it at the right time.

Sitting somewhere in California, thoughts arrive differently. The distance helps. The sky is different. The pace is different. I would read, think, talk to people I would not have otherwise talked to, disagree with things I had previously agreed with, agree with things I had previously dismissed. None of it for anyone. Just for the accumulation of becoming slightly less wrong.

That is what I want to protect. Not data. Thoughts.

Privacy Is Not About Hiding

Most people think privacy is about hiding something. This is the argument used against it — if you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear.

But that misunderstands what privacy actually protects.

Privacy protects the space in which thought happens before it becomes expression. The space between thinking and saying. If that space is observed, the thought changes. Not because you are dishonest — but because humans are social animals who modulate their expression based on audience. This is not a flaw. It is how we work.

When that modulation happens before the thought is finished — when the observation begins before the thinking does — the thought never forms cleanly. You start editing before you start thinking. The result is not authentic thought. It is performed thought.

The non-traceable ad is not just a preference. It is a signal that people understand, at an intuitive level, that observation changes behavior, and that unobserved thought is different from observed thought, and that the unobserved version is more truly theirs.

People prefer to have more privacy. This is not a political position. It is a human one.

The Arc I Watched

I came into technology through hardware — the physical substrate, the thing you hold. Then web development — interfaces, data moving through pipes. Then infrastructure — the pipes themselves, what makes the data move reliably at scale. Then blockchain — the question of who owns the data and who can see it. Then something stranger: the mathematical tools that change what "seeing" means.

ZK proofs. Fully homomorphic encryption. Threshold signatures. Multi-party computation.

What these tools do — at the mathematical level — is sever the connection between processing and knowing. I can compute on your data without knowing what your data is. I can prove I know something without revealing what I know. I can run a program on a secret and return only the output, with a proof that the program ran correctly, without ever touching the secret in plaintext.

This is not just a technical capability. It is the infrastructure for a different kind of relationship between people and the systems that serve them.

The direction is clear. We are moving toward a world where you do not have to choose between participation and privacy. Where the system can know enough to serve you without knowing enough to surveil you. Where the thought and the act can remain separate in a way that human social architecture rarely permits.

Theory Becomes Practice

When I started reading about zero-knowledge proofs — Groth16, PLONK, STARKs — they felt like pure mathematics. Beautiful proofs about what is and is not computable, what can and cannot be known, the theoretical limits of information.

Then I built axiom-engine. And the math became code. And the code became behavior. And the behavior was: an agent takes an action, and nobody needs to trust the agent, because the action comes with a proof. The proof is checkable. The trust is replaced by verification.

That shift — from theoretical to practical, from reading to building, from "this is possible in principle" to "this runs on a server right now" — is the same shift I watch happening in the broader relationship between humans and technology.

The theory that private computation is possible is decades old. The practice of private computation being deployed in production infrastructure is happening now. We learned. We evolved. The proofs turned into protocols, the protocols into systems, the systems into how things work.

That is how it always goes. Theory first. Then someone builds it. Then someone uses it. Then it is just the way the world works, and the next generation does not think of it as anything special.

I am trying to be one of the people who builds it.

What I Say Is Mine

This essay is personal. It does not belong to a company. It does not advance an agenda. It is not performing competence or optimizing for impressions.

It is what I actually think, written down, on a page I built, in a language I chose.

That is the point. The thoughts you have about your work, your life, your direction — whether private or public — belong to you. The choice of which to share and which to hold belongs to you. The infrastructure that enables that choice — encryption, ZK proofs, non-traceable data flows — is not a privacy tool in the narrow sense. It is freedom-of-thought infrastructure.

I build it because I believe it matters. And because the direction I see — more privacy, more verifiability, more sovereignty over one's own data and one's own thought — is the right direction.

The world is moving this way. I want to be someone who helped build the road.
